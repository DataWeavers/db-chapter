01

CHAPTER 1

Entities and relationships

Model the things a business needs to remember.

After reading, you should be able to
  • Distinguish a database, a DBMS and an application.
  • Identify entities, attributes, row grain and business rules.
  • Read relationships in both directions, including optionality and cardinality.

1.1 Why a business needs a data model

Consider a small bookstore. Its employees need to know which books are available, who has ordered them and what each customer agreed to pay. A manager may ask which categories are popular or whether a promotion increased order value. These questions require more than a large collection of facts. They require facts organized so that people can connect them consistently.

A database is an organized collection of data. A database management system (DBMS) is software that stores and retrieves that data and can enforce rules about it. An application is the interface someone uses, such as a checkout screen. The application asks the DBMS to read or change the database. A customer may never see the tables even though every order depends on them.

A data model describes what the business needs to remember and how those facts relate. Modeling comes before choosing screen layouts or writing queries. If two departments define “customer” differently, or if every order stores a different spelling of a book title, a faster query will not resolve the underlying disagreement.

DATABASE

The organized facts

Customer records, book records and orders.

DBMS

The software managing them

Accepts requests, stores changes and applies declared rules.

APPLICATION

The interface people use

A checkout page, order screen or reporting dashboard.

1.2 Entities, attributes and instances

An entity type is a kind of person, place, thing or event that the business needs to record. In a relational database, an entity type is usually represented by a table. An instance is one particular member of that type, represented by a row. An attribute is a fact describing an instance, represented by a column. “Customer” is an entity type; Avery Chen is one customer instance; City is one customer attribute.

Use the business questions to decide what deserves a table. Cedar Books needs to record customers, books and orders. It also needs to remember which books appear on each order. It does not need a separate table for every noun someone mentions. For a small system, City can be a customer attribute; a business managing city-level tax rules or delivery zones might model cities separately.

The running example: Cedar Books
TableOne row represents…Examples of attributes
CustomersOne registered customerCustomerName, City, Email
BooksOne sellable book editionTitle, Category, ListPrice
OrdersOne order placed by a customerOrderDate, Status
OrderLinesOne book edition on one orderQuantity, UnitPrice

The phrase “one row represents…” defines a table’s grain. Grain is the level of detail stored in the row. One order and one book on an order are different grains. If an order contains two books, it needs one Orders row and two OrderLines rows.

In this simplified model, a Books row means one sellable edition, not a physical copy and not every possible edition of the same title. If the business needed to track individual used copies with different conditions, those copies would need their own identifiers and a different grain. A useful model makes these choices explicit.

1.3 Put each fact where it belongs

Imagine recording everything in one spreadsheet row for each ordered book. An order with three different books would repeat the customer name and order date three times. If the customer changes their email, several rows might need updates. Missing one update creates disagreement about which email is correct.

Related tables reduce this avoidable repetition. Customer information belongs in Customers, order-level information belongs in Orders, and quantity belongs on an order line. A business can then combine those facts when it needs a receipt or a report.

Repetition is sometimes meaningful
A customer ID appearing on several orders is useful: it identifies who placed each order. Repeating a customer’s current email on every order creates multiple copies of the same fact. The distinction is between repeating a reference and repeating the fact it refers to.

1.4 Relationships express business rules

A relationship describes how instances are associated. “A customer places an order” connects a customer row to an order row. Describe each relationship in both directions: a customer may place many orders; each order belongs to one customer.

Two questions determine the limits. First, must an instance have a related instance, or can there be none? This is optionality, or the minimum. Second, can there be at most one related instance, or can there be many? This is the maximum cardinality. You will also hear “cardinality” used for the minimum–maximum pair.

Relationship patternMeaningExample with an explicit rule
One-to-oneAt most one match in each directionAn employee may have one assigned locker; a locker may be assigned to one employee.
One-to-manyOne instance can match many on the other sideA customer may place many orders; an order belongs to one customer.
Many-to-manyMultiple matches are possible in both directionsAn order can contain many books; a book can appear on many orders.

The relationship pattern describes the maximum. Optionality still needs to be stated. The employee–locker example allows an unassigned locker and an employee without a locker. A different organization might require every employee to have one.

1.5 Read an entity–relationship diagram

An entity–relationship diagram (ERD) uses boxes for entities and lines for relationships. In crow’s-foot notation, a circle means zero, a bar means one and a branching “foot” means many. Read the two symbols together at the end near the entity you are counting.

Zero or one0..1
Exactly one1..1
Zero or many0..many
One or many1..many

Cedar Books allows a customer to register before ordering, so a customer may have zero orders. Every stored order must belong to one registered customer, so the other end is exactly one. If guest orders were allowed without registration, that rule would need reconsideration.

The business rules determine the diagram. A small sample with one order per customer does not prove a one-to-one relationship. It shows what has happened so far, not everything the system must allow.

1.6 A practical modeling process

  1. Clarify the work. What must people record, and what questions must they answer?
  2. List candidate entities. Separate people, things and events that have their own facts.
  3. State the grain. Complete “one row represents…” for every table.
  4. Place attributes. Ask which particular thing each fact describes.
  5. Write relationships both ways. Include the minimum and maximum in each sentence.
  6. Test exceptions. Can someone register without buying? Can one order contain several books? Can two customers have the same name?

This process also works when reviewing a diagram suggested by AI. Translate each line back into two business-rule sentences. A neat diagram is useful only when those sentences match the business.

Check your understanding

Check your reasoning

No. A student can attend several lessons, and each lesson has its own date and tutor. Model lessons as separate records. A list of dates in one student cell makes individual lessons difficult to connect, update and count.

A bookstore allows people to create accounts before ordering. Must every Customers row already have an Orders row?

Check your reasoning

No. The minimum number of orders for a customer is zero. Each actual order can still be required to have exactly one customer; the two directions express different rules.

The idea to keep
A data model is a set of business definitions. Start by deciding what one row means, then make the allowed relationships explicit.

CHAPTER 2

Keys and connected tables

Give rows reliable identities and implement their relationships.

After reading, you should be able to
  • Choose primary keys and place foreign keys on the correct side.
  • Recognize when a composite key or a bridge table is needed.
  • Explain the rules keys enforce and why facts belong in separate tables.

2.1 A name is not an identity

Two customers can have the same name. A customer can also change their name, email or address. A database therefore needs a reliable way to identify the exact row being referenced. A key is a column, or combination of columns, that can uniquely identify a row.

A candidate key is a minimal set of columns that could uniquely identify every row: removing part of it would lose that guarantee. The designer chooses one candidate as the primary key (PK). A primary key must be unique and present for every row. A table has one primary-key constraint, but that constraint may contain several columns.

Two different customers named Morgan Lee
CustomerID · PKCustomerNameCity
1Avery ChenPortland

CustomerID 2 and CustomerID 4 identify different people even though both are named Morgan Lee. Looking up an order by customer name alone may mix their purchases. The ID keeps their records distinct.

A natural key already has a business meaning, such as a formally assigned product code. A surrogate key is an identifier created for the database, such as CustomerID. A surrogate key is often convenient when business identifiers may change, be unavailable or be entered inconsistently. It does not automatically prevent duplicate business records; the organization still needs rules for recognizing that two records describe the same person.

2.2 Foreign keys implement relationships

A foreign key (FK) is a column or set of columns that refers to a key in another table. It can also refer to the same table, as when an employee row identifies its manager. Most introductory examples reference another table’s primary key.

In a one-to-many relationship, place the foreign key on the many side. Each order stores one CustomerID. Customers does not need Order1, Order2 and Order3 columns, and it does not need a comma-separated list of order IDs.

Three orders and their customer references

Orders 501 and 503 both contain CustomerID 1. Repeating that FK is expected because Avery can place multiple orders. OrderID must be unique; CustomerID in Orders normally must not be unique, or each customer would be limited to one order.

Referential integrity means a nonmissing FK value has a matching referenced key. An order cannot point to CustomerID 999 if that customer does not exist. If the relationship is mandatory, the FK must also be required. If it is optional, a missing FK can represent “no related record.”

A matching column name does not create a relationship
Two columns named CustomerID are not automatically linked. The design must declare the foreign-key constraint. A query must also state its join condition; the DBMS does not infer every join merely because an FK exists.

2.3 Composite keys identify combinations

A composite key uses two or more columns together. Cedar Books allows each book edition to appear once per order; Quantity records how many copies were ordered. OrderID alone is not unique in OrderLines, because an order can contain several books. BookID alone is not unique either, because a book can appear on several orders. The pair (OrderID, BookID) identifies one line.

The pair (501, 101) can appear only once. The same book may appear in (506, 101), and the same order may also contain (501, 102). These are different pairs, so both are allowed.

A composite key must fit the business rule. If the same book could appear twice on an order with different discounts, this pair would no longer be enough. One alternative is (OrderID, LineNumber); another is a separate OrderLineID. Key selection starts with the meaning of a row.

2.4 Resolve many-to-many relationships with a bridge

Orders and Books have a many-to-many relationship: one order can include several books, and one book can appear on several orders. A relational design represents that relationship using an associative entity, also called a bridge or composite entity. Each bridge row records one pairing.

OrderLines is that bridge. It turns the many-to-many relationship into two one-to-many relationships: Orders → OrderLines and Books → OrderLines. Quantity and UnitPrice belong to the pairing. A book does not have one universal “quantity ordered”; that quantity depends on the order.

“Composite entity” and “composite key” describe different ideas. The first is a table representing an association. The second is an identifier using multiple columns. A bridge may use a separate primary-key ID. If repeated pairs are forbidden, that design also needs a UNIQUE constraint on the pair.

2.5 Understand what keys do—and what they do not do

Primary and foreign keys enforce specific rules. A primary key rejects a repeated identifier. A foreign key rejects a reference to a nonexistent parent when FK enforcement is active. Neither guarantees that every business statement is true.

RuleHow it can be enforced
Every order has an existing customerA required CustomerID FK in Orders.
The same book appears at most once per orderA composite PK on (OrderID, BookID), or an equivalent UNIQUE rule.
Every order contains at least one lineAdditional transaction, application or database logic; the child FK alone is insufficient.
Quantity is a positive whole numberA suitable data type plus validation constraints.
At most one locker is assigned to an employeeA UNIQUE rule on the assignment FK, in addition to the FK itself.

Think about deletion too. If a customer has orders, deleting the customer could leave orphaned references. A database can reject the deletion or apply a deliberately chosen rule, such as deleting dependent records or making an optional reference missing. Removing historical orders automatically is often inappropriate for business records. The correct behavior depends on the information the business needs to preserve.

2.6 Organize facts to avoid conflicting copies

Normalization is a set of principles for organizing relational tables so that each fact is stored in an appropriate place. It reduces update anomalies (one fact must be changed in several places), insertion anomalies (a fact cannot be added without an unrelated fact), and deletion anomalies (deleting one record accidentally removes the only record of another fact).

For example, if the only place a book’s title appears is on order lines, the store cannot record a new book before its first order without creating an artificial order. Deleting its last order might also erase the only record of the book. A separate Books table avoids both problems.

A first look at the first three normal forms

These are introductory working rules for this example:

  1. First normal form: use one value per cell at the intended level of detail and avoid repeating groups such as Book1, Book2 and Book3. Put order lines in their own rows.
  2. Second normal form: non-key attributes should depend on the whole of every candidate key, not just part of a composite key. In OrderLines, Title depends on BookID alone, so it belongs in Books.
  3. Third normal form: avoid storing a non-key fact that is determined through another non-key fact. CustomerName depends on CustomerID; it should not be repeated in Orders as though it were an order fact.

These rules concern dependencies, not simply the number of columns. More tables are useful when they separate distinct facts, not merely because small tables look tidy.

Historical facts require care. Books.ListPrice is the current price. OrderLines.UnitPrice is the price agreed for that line. They may legitimately differ. Replacing the historical unit price with today’s list price would lose information about the transaction.

Choose the correct side

A repair shop records technicians and jobs. Each job has one lead technician; one technician can lead several jobs. Where should the FK go?

Check your reasoning

Put TechnicianID in Jobs. Jobs is the many side, and each job needs one reference to its lead technician. Requiring the FK makes the relationship mandatory.

Spot the key error

An OrderLines table uses BookID alone as its PK. What valid business event would it reject?

Check your reasoning

A later order for a book that has already appeared on any earlier order. BookID alone would allow each book only one line in the entire database. The pair (OrderID, BookID) supports the stated one-book-per-order rule.

Separate the two prices

A book’s list price rises from $25 to $30. Should older order lines be changed to $30?

Check your reasoning

No. Their UnitPrice records what was agreed at the time. Update the current ListPrice in Books while retaining the historical transaction values.

The idea to keep
A key identifies a row; a foreign key refers to a row. A bridge records an association. Keeping these roles clear makes both the design and its queries easier to reason about.

CHAPTER 3

Metadata and data dictionaries

Make the meaning of the data explicit enough to build and use it.

After reading, you should be able to
  • Document types, units, required values, keys and business meanings.
  • Distinguish missing values, defaults and validation rules.
  • Read CREATE TABLE and compare an implemented schema with its dictionary.

3.1 A column name is not a complete definition

Suppose two reports both contain a column named Amount. One measures orders placed; another measures invoices issued; a third measures money received. The values may all be accurate and still answer different questions. A clear name helps, but the business also needs a shared definition.

Metadata is information describing data. A data dictionary is a structured reference for those definitions. It records what each column means, how values are represented, which values are allowed, and whether a value can be missing. It helps designers build tables, employees enter data and analysts interpret results.

Useful dictionary entries include the table and column name, business meaning, data type, units or format, required status, default value if any, key role, and validation rules. A larger organization may also record the source system, responsible owner and sensitivity of the data.

Weak definitionMore useful definition
UnitPrice: the priceActual price per copy agreed for this order line, in dollars, before tax; not the current list price.
OrderDate: dateCalendar date on which the order was placed, stored as YYYY-MM-DD; not the shipping date.
Quantity: a numberPositive whole number of copies on this order line; not the number currently in stock.

3.2 Choose a type that matches the meaning

A data type describes the kind of value a column is intended to hold. Common choices include whole numbers, decimal numbers, text, dates and Boolean values. The exact type names vary among database systems. The executable examples in this reading use SQLite, which keeps setup small; the underlying design concepts apply more broadly.

MeaningSQLite representation used hereExample

Choose by meaning, not appearance. A postal code or employee code made entirely of digits may still belong in TEXT. Leading zeros can matter, and adding two postal codes is meaningless. A quantity is different: arithmetic on it is meaningful.

Type and format are also different. A stored numeric value of 14.5 can be displayed as $14.50 without storing the dollar sign. A date can have a consistent storage format even if an application displays it differently. For these examples, fixed-width year–month–day dates also sort in calendar order.

A small SQLite distinction
SQLite normally uses flexible typing. Declaring a column INTEGER does not by itself express every business rule. The complete example schema adds checks for positive whole-number quantities and numeric prices. REAL uses approximate arithmetic; applications needing exact monetary arithmetic often use integer cents or a suitable exact decimal type. These small examples round displayed totals. 3.3 Required values, missing values and defaults

A required column must have a value. SQL expresses this with NOT NULL. An optional value may be NULL, meaning that the value is missing or unknown. NULL is not the number zero, the word “NULL” or an empty string.

Cedar Books allows Email to be missing because customers can order without providing one. It requires Quantity because an order line without a quantity cannot be interpreted. Setting a missing quantity to zero would change its meaning and conceal the missing information.

A default supplies a value when a column is omitted from an insert. It should reflect a valid business rule, such as a new request starting with status Pending. A default is not a substitute for an unknown fact. Giving every missing city the default “Portland” would manufacture information.

Required does not necessarily mean meaningful. NOT NULL allows an empty string in a text column. A system may need further validation for blank names, valid calendar dates, plausible email formats and consistent categories. A dictionary describes the intended rule; the implementation must state which part is enforced where.

3.5 Translate definitions into CREATE TABLE

SQL can define a database’s structure as well as retrieve data. CREATE TABLE names a table and lists its columns and constraints. The collection of table definitions and relationships is the database’s schema.

Creating Customers
CREATE TABLE Customers (
  CustomerID INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  CustomerName TEXT NOT NULL,
  City TEXT NOT NULL,
  Email TEXT
);

Each line defines a column. CustomerID is the primary key. CustomerName and City are required. Email has no NOT NULL constraint, so it may be missing. Commas separate definitions; the closing parenthesis and semicolon finish the statement.

Creating Orders and its customer relationship
CREATE TABLE Orders (
  OrderID INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  CustomerID INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES Customers(CustomerID),
  OrderDate TEXT NOT NULL,
  Status TEXT NOT NULL
    CHECK (Status IN ('Completed', 'Pending', 'Cancelled'))
);

REFERENCES declares the FK. CHECK restricts Status to the listed values. A value can be text of the correct type and still be invalid—for example, “Nearly finished” is not an allowed status here. Parent and child key columns use matching types.

A composite key is written at table level as PRIMARY KEY (OrderID, BookID). This declares one key containing two columns. Writing a separate PRIMARY KEY on each column would not mean the same thing.

  OrderID INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES Orders(OrderID),
  BookID INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES Books(BookID),
  Quantity INTEGER NOT NULL
    CHECK (typeof(Quantity) = 'integer' AND Quantity > 0),
  UnitPrice REAL NOT NULL

  PRIMARY KEY (OrderID, BookID)
);

typeof checks the actual stored value’s type in SQLite. Together with the numeric comparisons, these checks reject text prices, nonpositive quantities and fractional quantities. The schema does not validate email syntax or whether OrderDate is a real calendar date. Those remain explicitly documented validation requirements.

In SQLite, enable foreign-key enforcement for each connection before transactions with PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON;. A successful CREATE TABLE statement establishes the declared structure; it does not demonstrate that all requirements have been covered. Foreign-key reference ↗

3.6 Test the rules, not just the syntax

Try valid and invalid records in a disposable practice database. A valid order for an existing customer should be accepted. An order referencing a nonexistent customer should fail. A repeated primary key, a missing required name and a negative quantity should fail for different reasons. These tests show whether the intended rules are actually in effect.

Then compare the schema with the dictionary line by line. A database may happily create Email as a required number or omit an FK if that is what its SQL says. It checks the instructions it was given, not the business rules that were left out. The same comparison is essential when a person or an AI tool drafts the schema.

Check your understanding

Protect the identifier

A supplier’s account code is 004218 and must retain all six characters. Should it be stored as INTEGER or TEXT?

Check your reasoning

TEXT. It is a code, and the leading zeros are meaningful. An arithmetic number type is not a good representation of that requirement.

Required is not unique

If CustomerName is TEXT NOT NULL, does that prevent two customers named Morgan Lee?

Check your reasoning

No. NOT NULL requires a value; it does not make the value unique. A primary key or UNIQUE rule expresses uniqueness. In this example, duplicate names are allowed and CustomerID distinguishes the rows.

A dictionary definition that changes a report

Why must the dictionary distinguish ListPrice from UnitPrice?

Check your reasoning

ListPrice describes the book now. UnitPrice describes a particular sale agreement. Using ListPrice to calculate past order value can misstate discounts or price changes even when the SQL runs correctly.

The idea to keep
The dictionary explains what the data means. The schema implements selected rules. Trustworthy data requires agreement between the definitions, constraints and business process.

CHAPTER 4

Asking questions with SQL

Turn a precise business question into a result you can explain.

After reading, you should be able to
  • Choose, sort and filter rows, including combinations of conditions.
  • Calculate values and summarize them at the intended grain.
  • Join tables correctly and explain why records without matches are omitted.

4.1 Define the question before writing SQL

SQL (Structured Query Language) is a language for working with relational databases. A query is a request for data. The SELECT queries in this chapter read records and produce a result table; they do not change the stored data.

A business question needs a precise definition before it becomes a query. “How are sales doing?” leaves several decisions open: which dates, which order statuses, which measure, and which level of detail? “What was the merchandise value of completed orders placed in April?” is much clearer. It still needs a definition of the price to use and any exclusions, such as tax or shipping.

For every query, decide the population (which records qualify), the grain (what one output row represents), the columns or measures needed, and the intended sort order. Those decisions guide the SQL and give you a way to check its answer.

Everything needed for these examples is included
The examples use the fictional Cedar Books data, not a live business. You can read the queries and result tables without installing anything. The complete sample data, SQLite database and setup SQL are available below for optional hands-on practice. These queries use SQLite syntax; small syntax details vary among DBMSs.

4.2 SELECT chooses columns; FROM chooses the source

Start with the book catalog. The question is: “What are the book titles and current list prices, from highest price to lowest?” One output row should represent one book.

Book catalog by price
SELECT Title, ListPrice
FROM Books
ORDER BY ListPrice DESC, Title;

SELECT lists the requested columns, separated by commas. FROM names their source table. ORDER BY controls the result’s order. DESC means descending, so the largest price appears first. If two prices tie, Title supplies a second sort criterion. Ascending order is the default; you may write ASC explicitly.

SQL keywords are shown in uppercase to make them easy to see; uppercase is a style choice. A semicolon ends a statement. A line beginning with -- is a comment for the reader. Table and column names must match the schema.

SELECT * requests all columns and can help you inspect an unfamiliar table. For a report, name the columns you need so the result has a clear purpose. Without ORDER BY, do not assume rows will appear in a particular sequence.

4.3 WHERE keeps the rows that pass a test

To find business books priced above $20, add a condition. Each Books row is tested, and only rows for which the complete condition is true remain.

Business books above $20
SELECT BookID, Title, ListPrice
FROM Books
WHERE Category = 'Business' AND ListPrice > 20
ORDER BY Title;
Result
BookIDTitleListPrice
103Practical Data30.0

Text values use single quotes, as in 'Business'. Numeric values do not need quotes. Common comparisons are = (equal), <> (not equal), >, >=, < and <=. Above $20 excludes a $20 book; at least $20 includes it. That small wording change changes the query.

4.4 Use AND, OR and parentheses deliberately

AND requires every connected condition to pass. OR requires at least one. Suppose the request is “business or fiction books costing at least $20.” The price condition must apply to both categories.

A price condition applied to two categories
SELECT Title, Category, ListPrice
FROM Books
WHERE ListPrice >= 20
  AND (Category = 'Business' OR Category = 'Fiction')
ORDER BY Title;
Result
TitleCategoryListPrice
Everyday EconomicsBusiness20.0
Practical DataBusiness30.0

Without the parentheses, the condition ListPrice >= 20 AND Category = 'Business' OR Category = 'Fiction' includes every fiction book, including the $15 book. SQL gives AND precedence over OR. Parentheses express the intended grouping and make the logic easier to review.

Repeat the column name in each comparison. Do not write Category = 'Business' OR 'Fiction': the second value is not a complete comparison.

4.5 Patterns and date ranges

LIKE matches a text pattern. In Title LIKE '%Data%', each percent sign represents any number of characters, so the pattern finds titles containing Data. Title LIKE 'The%' finds titles beginning with The. The underscore character represents exactly one character. Matching case can depend on the DBMS and its settings.

For a date range, fixed-format YYYY-MM-DD values allow a straightforward comparison. This example includes April 1 and excludes May 1, making the entire month explicit.

Orders placed in April
SELECT OrderID, OrderDate, Status
FROM Orders
WHERE OrderDate >= '2025-04-01'
  AND OrderDate < '2025-05-01'
ORDER BY OrderDate, OrderID;

4.6 Calculated columns turn inputs into measures

A calculated column evaluates an expression for each result row. For order lines, multiplying Quantity by UnitPrice gives the line’s merchandise value. AS gives that expression a readable output name, called an alias.

Calculate the two line values on order 501
SELECT OrderID, BookID, Quantity, UnitPrice,
       ROUND(Quantity * UnitPrice, 2) AS LineValue
FROM OrderLines
WHERE OrderID = 501
ORDER BY BookID;

4.7 Aggregates summarize many rows

An aggregate function summarizes a set of values. COUNT counts, SUM adds, AVG calculates an arithmetic mean, and MIN and MAX find the smallest and largest values. Without GROUP BY, the following query summarizes the whole Books table into one row.

Summarize the catalog
SELECT COUNT(*) AS NumberOfBooks,
       ROUND(AVG(ListPrice), 2) AS AverageListPrice,
       MIN(ListPrice) AS LowestPrice,
       MAX(ListPrice) AS HighestPrice
FROM Books;
Result
NumberOfBooksAverageListPriceLowestPriceHighestPrice
521.415.030.0

Check the arithmetic: the five list prices are 20, 15, 30, 24 and 18. Their sum is 107, so the average is 107 ÷ 5 = 21.4. The result describes book editions in the catalog, not copies sold or average order value.

COUNT(*) counts rows. In Customers, it counts four customers; in OrderLines, it counts nine lines. To count orders, query Orders at the order grain. A join to order lines changes what a row represents, so its row count cannot automatically be called an order count.

SUM answers a different question from COUNT. For order 501, there are two lines, three copies, and $50 of merchandise value:

Lines, copies and order value are different measures
SELECT COUNT(*) AS Lines,
       SUM(Quantity) AS Copies,
       ROUND(SUM(Quantity * UnitPrice), 2) AS OrderValue
FROM OrderLines
WHERE OrderID = 501;
Result
LinesCopiesOrderValue
2350.0

AVG, SUM, MIN and MAX normally ignore NULL inputs. An empty qualifying set gives COUNT of zero, while SUM and AVG yield NULL in SQLite. Decide whether “no matching records” should be reported as missing, zero, or a separate message; do not let display formatting silently make that decision.

4.8 GROUP BY changes the output grain

A manager may need one summary for each category rather than one summary for the whole catalog. GROUP BY divides the qualifying rows into groups before the aggregates are calculated. The output grain becomes one row per category.

One summary per category
SELECT Category, COUNT(*) AS NumberOfBooks,
       ROUND(AVG(ListPrice), 2) AS AverageListPrice
FROM Books
GROUP BY Category
ORDER BY Category;
Result
CategoryNumberOfBooksAverageListPrice
Business225.0
Cooking118.0
Fiction115.0
Travel124.0

The Business group contains two books, with prices 20 and 30, so its average is 25. The other groups each contain one book. Their counts add to five, matching the number of catalog rows. This simple reconciliation helps catch mistakes.

As a portable beginner rule, every selected column should either be aggregated or appear in GROUP BY. Asking for Category, Title and AVG(ListPrice) while grouping only by Category is ambiguous: which title should represent a category containing several books? Some systems reject the query; SQLite may return a value that does not mean what you intended.

4.9 JOIN reconstructs a connected business record

Separating facts into related tables does not prevent us from viewing them together. A join combines rows according to a matching condition. Here, an Orders row finds its customer by comparing their CustomerID values.

Attach the customer name to an order
SELECT o.OrderID, o.OrderDate, c.CustomerName
FROM Orders AS o
JOIN Customers AS c ON c.CustomerID = o.CustomerID
WHERE o.OrderID = 501;
Result
OrderIDOrderDateCustomerName
5012025-04-03Avery Chen

AS o gives Orders the short alias o; AS c gives Customers the alias c. The expression c.CustomerID = o.CustomerID follows the relationship. A qualified name such as o.OrderID tells SQL exactly which table supplies a column.

An ordinary JOIN here is an inner join: it returns matches from both tables. Joining one order to its single customer leaves one row per order. Joining that order to its two lines changes the grain to one row per order line.

Reconstruct the details of order 501
SELECT o.OrderID, c.CustomerName, b.Title,
       ol.Quantity, ol.UnitPrice,
       ROUND(ol.Quantity * ol.UnitPrice, 2) AS LineValue
FROM Orders AS o
JOIN Customers AS c ON c.CustomerID = o.CustomerID
JOIN OrderLines AS ol ON ol.OrderID = o.OrderID
JOIN Books AS b ON b.BookID = ol.BookID
WHERE o.OrderID = 501
ORDER BY b.BookID;

Seeing Avery’s name twice is expected because the result has two lines from the same order. The query has not created two customers or two orders. Each line is matched to one customer, one order and one book through their keys.

Every join needs a reason. Joining Books to OrderLines on an unrelated field can produce the wrong matches or multiply rows. A missing matching condition can combine every row from one source with every row from another. Check the resulting grain and count before trusting a total.

4.10 Summarize joined rows and account for missing matches

To count the orders on file for each customer with orders, join Customers to Orders and group by the customer's ID and name. After this join, each working row represents one order, so COUNT(*) counts orders within each customer group.

Orders on file for customers with matching orders
SELECT c.CustomerID, c.CustomerName, COUNT(*) AS OrdersOnFile
FROM Customers AS c
JOIN Orders AS o ON o.CustomerID = c.CustomerID
GROUP BY c.CustomerID, c.CustomerName
ORDER BY c.CustomerID;

This counts all statuses because the question asks for orders on file. There are four registered customers, but only three result rows. CustomerID 4 has no matching order and is omitted by the join. An absent customer in this result is not a displayed zero; the query returns only customers with matches.

Group by CustomerID as well as CustomerName so different customers with the same name are not combined. Names are labels, not guaranteed identifiers. Always ask whether a report is meant to include every registered customer or only those with matching transactions.

4.11 Read a query in two useful orders

The written order of the clauses is consistent:

SELECT→FROM / JOIN→WHERE→GROUP BY→ORDER BY

For understanding the result, follow a conceptual path: identify the source rows and joins, apply row filters, form any groups, calculate the requested output, and sort it. This is a reasoning aid, not a promise about the DBMS’s physical execution plan.

Then ask whether the result answers the original question. Does each row represent what you intended? Did you count orders or lines? Does the status filter match the business definition? Can you reproduce one amount by hand? SQL syntax and business interpretation need separate checks.

CHAPTER 5

Checking AI-drafted SQL

Evaluate the answer against the business meaning and the underlying records.

After reading, you should be able to
  • Provide a precise question, real schema and relevant definitions to a drafting tool.
  • Use count, grain, total, spot-check and schema checks to audit a query.
  • Report a defensible business answer with its assumptions and limits.

5.1 Running successfully is only one kind of correctness

An AI assistant can turn a plain-language request into an SQL draft, explain syntax or suggest a join. That can reduce the effort of getting started. It does not transfer responsibility for the business definition or the answer.

A query can be wrong in several ways. It may contain a syntax error, refer to a nonexistent column, join the wrong records, include the wrong population or calculate the wrong measure. The DBMS often catches the first two. It usually cannot know that you meant completed orders instead of all orders, or that a reported count is supposed to mean orders rather than lines.

Even a plausible answer is weak evidence. A total of $253 may look reasonable when the correct total is $177. What matters is whether the answer can be explained and reconciled to the records.

5.2 Give the draft a clear contract

Provide the real schema, relevant dictionary definitions and a precise question. Specify the desired output grain, date boundaries, statuses to include, and the meaning of each measure. If a needed field is absent, the tool should identify the gap rather than invent a column.

For Cedar Books, “April sales” is too vague. The intended request is: How many completed orders were placed in April 2025, and what was their total merchandise value at the unit prices recorded on the lines? We need two summary numbers: an order count and a total value. Pending and Cancelled orders are excluded.

An example prompt for this question
Prompt for an SQL draft
Write a read-only SQLite SELECT query using the schema below.

Question: How many distinct completed orders were placed in
April 2025, and what was their total merchandise order value?

Use two simple SELECT queries: one to count orders directly
from Orders, and one to sum the value of their matching lines.
Include Status = 'Completed'. Exclude Pending and Cancelled.
Use dates from April 1 up to, but not including, May 1.
Value each line as Quantity * OrderLines.UnitPrice.
Do not use the current Books.ListPrice for historical order value.
Exclude tax and shipping; do not describe the result as cash received.

Explain each join and the grain before and after aggregation.

If a needed field is missing, identify the gap rather than inventing it.

[Include the actual table definitions and relevant dictionary entries.]

The prompt’s assumptions are part of the answer. “Completed” is the example’s order status; it does not prove that a payment has been received. The schema has no payment table. A query over these tables can report order value, but cannot establish cash collected.

These checks reinforce one another. A count can expose a row-multiplying join. A spot-check can expose the use of today’s price. The dictionary can explain why that price is inappropriate. No single check proves every aspect of correctness.

5.4 A worked audit: a query that runs but answers the wrong question

Suppose an AI assistant drafts the following query for the April completed-order question. This illustrative draft contains three common errors.

Illustrative draft with errors

       ROUND(SUM(ol.Quantity * b.ListPrice), 2) AS OrderValue
FROM Orders AS o
JOIN OrderLines AS ol ON ol.OrderID = o.OrderID

Also test exclusions. Order 503 is Cancelled and order 505 is Pending, so neither belongs in the completed-order population. Order 506 is Completed but dated May 2, so it belongs outside April. For a larger dataset, include records at the start and end of the date range and cases with no matches or missing optional values.

Check 5 · Compare every reference with the schema

The joins from Orders to OrderLines and from OrderLines to Books use valid keys. That is necessary, but it does not make the whole draft correct. The dictionary says ListPrice is current and UnitPrice is historical. Status is a separate column whose allowed values define the intended filter. Orders supports the order count at one row per order; OrderLines supplies Quantity and UnitPrice for the value calculation.

The final query does not need Books, because the requested measures are available in Orders and OrderLines. Removing an unnecessary join makes the logic easier to inspect.

5.5 Use two clear queries, then state the business answer

Keep the calculations at their appropriate grains. The first query counts qualifying order rows. The second joins those orders to their lines and sums the recorded line values. Both use the same status and date filters.

Final answer: count completed orders

The value query depends on the example’s rule that completed orders have order lines. An inner join would omit an order with no lines. The order-subtotal result should contain the same three order IDs counted in the base table. Comparing those records helps reveal such an inconsistency. A well-designed check pays attention both to the query and to the quality of the underlying data.

When using an AI-drafted query, preserve the business question, the query you actually ran, the important assumptions and a short account of the checks. This makes the result reviewable and easier to update when requirements change.

Check your understanding

Find the hidden population change

Neither. The rows are lines. In this example they represent three orders and ten copies. Count qualifying rows in Orders for the order count, and sum Quantity in the qualifying lines for copies.

A wider view: transactional databases and warehouses

The tables in this reading support everyday transactions: recording an order, identifying its customer and retaining its line details. A data warehouse brings together data for analysis, often from several operational systems and over longer periods.

A common analytical design is a star schema. A fact table records events or measurements at a clearly defined grain, while dimension tables describe subjects such as product, customer, store and date. A fact table at the sales-line grain might contain quantity and amount, linked to those dimensions.

The central questions remain the same: what does one row represent, how do tables connect, and what does each measure mean? Aggregating before checking those definitions can produce convincing but misleading reports in a warehouse just as easily as in a small operational database.
